# The Big Three

The big three, that is Stella Adler, Sanford Meisner and Lee Strasberg were all original members of the New York Group Theatre in the 1930s. While they we're all trained in Stanislavski's system, they had different visions of how to interpret it. This split led to the cornerstones of modern acting in the western world.

---

## Lee Strasberg 

*Emotional Memory*

Known widely today as *The Method*, Strasberg focused primarily on emotional recall as outlined by early Stanislavski. Instead of looking outward to other actors or imagination, actors are encouraged to dig inward, where past experiences provide everything needed for emotional authenticity. It is essentially a subset of Stanislavski's system, focusing entirely on emotional recall.

### Affective Memory Exercise

Starting in a blank state (relaxation exercise), the actor sits alone in a room and recalls an emotional event from their past. Senses can be activated using guiding question like "What did the room smell like?", "What kind of fabric where you wearing?", "What was the temperature like?".

### 5 Step Approach

1. Relaxation Exercise: Become a blank canvas. Sit in a chair and release all physical tension while making a vocal sound.
2. Sense Memory: Recall a sensory stimulus like feeling raindrops in your face, or hearing city noise in the background.
3. Affective Memory: Recall a specific emotional event from your past.
4. Substitution: Map a situation in the script with a fitting emotional event from your past.
5. Script: Deliver the script while keeping the emotion from the recall burning.

*"If you don't feel it, don't fake it."*

Strasberg solves the problem of fake, overly theatrical acting by generating raw internal emotion using personal memories (Affective/Emotional Memory).

## Stella Adler 

*The power of imagination.*

Stella Adler broke away from Lee's Strasberg's approach after studying directly under Stanislavski in Paris. Adler hated the idea of recalling trauma just to develop a role, and by that time late Stanislavski has reached the same conclusion. It can be seen as a mature version of late Stanislavski, that never made it back to the US.

The Adler technique focuses on imagination over emotional recall. Acting is doing. Emotion is evoked naturally by physical movement through the imaginary world. Instead of relying on the director, actors should deeply study the script, analyze social relationships and read between the lines. Adler can be seen as the most intellectual and theoretical school of the big three.

*What if?* What exactly does it smell like, feel like, look like in exactly this hypothetical situation? Go into detail. (similar to Stanislavski).

### Paraphrase Exercise

After reading a script, but before knowing it by heart, the actor chooses a specific section. Then, the actor delivers the exact meaning, logic and passion of this section. The goal is to experience the meaning behind the words.

### 3 Step Approach

1. Script: Study and analyse the Script deeply. Read between the lines.
2. Imagination: Construct a vivid world. What does it smell like, feel like, look like?
3. Action: Start Moving, focus on what the character is doing, and let the emotion come to you through the movement.

*"The imagination is the ignition key. Without it, nothing else works. Your talent is in your choice."*

Adler solves the problem of limited range and mental health impact by training the actor’s imagination and script research so they don't have to rely on their own life experiences.

## Sanford Meisner

*Behave truthfully under imaginary circumstances.*

Instead of looking inward for emotional recall, the goal is to look outward to the other actors. Based on Stanislavski's behavioural branch, it focuses on becoming authentic in the moment through improvisation exercises. The technique is prioritising raw instinct, active listening, and spontaneity. Emotion is drawn from vivid imagination (painting the world around) instead of emotional recall. Acting should be pure reaction.

### Repetition Exercise

Two actors sit opposite of each other and start with an objective observation about the other person, like "You have blond hair". The other person repeats with "I have blond hair". After several rounds the actors progress onto behavioural and even emotional observations, like "You're crossing your arms", "You didn't like that I said that". The goal of the exercise is to stop thinking, to destroy self-censorship and to open up in order to reach flow state.

*"To be an interesting actor, you must be authentic. For you to ever be authentic, you must embrace who you really are. Do you have any idea how liberating it is to not care what people think about you? Well, that's what we're here to do."*

### 5 Step Approach

1. Repetition Exercise: Stop thinking and open up.
2. Add Behaviour: Repeat the line until the partner changes body or voice. 
3. Add Action: Improv Exercise, both characters have an urgent, opposed task to fulfil
4. Add Emotion: Start Blank (relaxation exercise) then leverage imagination to enter the emotional state (anger, sadness, etc)
5. Add Script: Instead of repeating, say a line from the script. Study the lines flat (emotionless) and let the emotion from the exercise carry it.

*"Act before you think – your instincts are more honest than your thoughts."* 

Meisner solves the problem of overthinking, stiffness, and self-consciousness by shifting focus entirely to active listening and the present moment. The exercises are designed to pull you out of your head and onto the stage in the present.
